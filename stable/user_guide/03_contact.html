Contact between cells#

Cells interact only where their surfaces come close: they adhere, and they repel each other where they overlap. SimuCell3D offers three contact models, chosen at compile time with CONTACT_MODEL_INDEX. This chapter describes how contacts are found, the force law or coupling rule of each model, the parameters involved, and the limitations that matter in practice.

Concept#

The three models differ in how adhesion is transmitted:

Index

Name

Adhesion

Repulsion

Described in the paper

0

node-face springs

bilinear spring stress between a node and a face, strength \(\omega\)

linear spring stress, strength \(\xi\)

yes (“spring-based model”)

1

node-node coupling

a node is tied to one node of another epithelial cell

linear spring stress, strength \(\xi\)

yes (“coupling-based model”)

2

face-face coupling (default)

a node is tied to one node on each neighbouring epithelial cell

linear spring stress, strength \(\xi\)

no; extension of model 1

Models 1 and 2 replace a finite adhesion stiffness by a rigid tie between nodes, so contact mechanics do not depend on an adhesion strength. Runser, Vetter & Iber (2024) validate the coupling model against the Young-Dupré relation for cell doublets and triplets (Supplementary Fig. 5) and report that the spring model depends somewhat on mesh resolution (Supplementary Fig. 4). Model 2 is the default of 2.x and of the tag v1.0; the Zenodo archive of 1.0 cited by the paper has model 1 as its default (Release 1.0). The parameter files for the vesicle, sheet, tube and growth examples are written for model 2.

Finding contacts#

All three models test nodes of one cell against faces of other cells. Every face gets an axis-aligned bounding box enlarged by the contact range \(c = \max(c_a, c_r)\), where \(c_a\) is contact_cutoff_adhesion and \(c_r\) is contact_cutoff_repulsion. A node is a contact candidate for a face of another cell if it lies in that box. For each candidate the solver computes the closest point \(\mathbf r_\mathrm{cp}\) of the triangle to the node, its barycentric coordinates \((\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3)\), and the vector \(\mathbf z = \mathbf r_\mathrm{node} - \mathbf r_\mathrm{cp}\), of length \(d = \lVert\mathbf z\rVert\).

The side of the face decides the type of contact. A node in front of the face (\(\mathbf z \cdot \hat{\mathbf n}_f > 0\)) is outside the other cell and can adhere; a node behind it is inside the other cell and is repelled. Two pairs of cell classes swap the two sides, because one surface encloses the other: a node of an epithelial cell against a face of the ECM (the ECM encloses the tissue), and a node of a nucleus against a face of an epithelial cell (the nucleus lies inside the cell).

Candidates are found with a uniform grid or with Sweep-and-Prune (see Contact detection algorithms below).

Model 0: node-face springs#

The contact stress follows the bilinear traction-separation law of the paper’s Eq. (2). With \(d\) the distance between node and face,

(1)#\[\begin{split} \sigma(d) = \begin{cases} \xi\, d & \text{node behind the face, } d < c_r \quad \text{(repulsion)}\\ \omega\, d & \text{node in front of the face, } d < c_a/2 \quad \text{(adhesion, rising)}\\ \omega\, (c_a - d) & \text{node in front of the face, } c_a/2 \le d < c_a \quad \text{(adhesion, falling)}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \end{split}\]

The stress acts over the area \(A_f\) of the face, which gives the force \(\mathbf F = \sigma(d)\,A_f\,\mathbf z/d\). The node receives \(-\mathbf F\), which points towards the face in both regimes: for adhesion it pulls the node onto the other cell, for repulsion it pushes the node back out of it. The three nodes of the face receive \(+\mathbf F\), shared with weights \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3\), so momentum is conserved. \(\omega\) and \(\xi\) are taken from the face type of the face that is touched. Every node of every cell is tested against the faces of all other cells, so each contact is seen from both sides.

Note

The paper’s Methods state the law for pairs of faces \(\{f_a, f_b\}\) and the contact area \(A_{ab}\). The code applies it to node-face pairs with the area of the face.

With polarization mode 1, model 0 also labels the touched face as apical, lateral or basal from the class of the touching cell (Polarization).

Model 1: node-node coupling#

Model 1 couples nodes of adjacent epithelial cells. For a node \(n_1\) of an epithelial cell and a candidate face of another epithelial cell, the closest node \(n_2\) of that face is coupled to \(n_1\) if all of the following hold:

  • the distance between \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) is below \(c_a\) and below the distance of any partner \(n_1\) already has;

  • \(n_2\) has no closer partner;

  • the node normals are at least 45 degrees apart (\(\hat{\mathbf n}_1 \cdot \hat{\mathbf n}_2 \le \cos 45^\circ\)), and the node normal of \(n_1\) points against the face normal;

  • the mean curvature at both nodes is below surface_coupling_max_curvature \(H_{\max}\) of the cell type of \(n_1\).

A node has at most one partner. Coupled nodes are moved to their common midpoint \((\mathbf r_1 + \mathbf r_2)/2\), and during time integration they share their averaged force and momentum, so they move together (Time integration). This transmits tension directly from one cell surface to the next, as in the paper’s coupling-based model. Couplings are recomputed at every iteration, which lets cells detach.

A node-face test that does not create a coupling falls through to repulsion. This covers every contact with a non-epithelial cell, and a test between epithelial cells that finds no closer partner, even when the node already has a partner: a node behind a face, closer than \(\max(c_a, c_r)\), receives the repulsive stress \(\xi d\) of (1). The adherence strength \(\omega\) is not used by model 1.

In models 1 and 2 two tests on the node \(n_1\) come before any of this, for coupling and for repulsion alike: its mean curvature must be below \(H_{\max}\) of its own cell type, and its node normal must point against the face normal (\(\hat{\mathbf n}_1 \cdot \hat{\mathbf n}_f < 0\)). A node that fails either test receives no contact force from that face. Model 0 has neither test.

Model 2: face-face coupling#

Model 2, the default, generalises model 1 so that a node can be coupled to one node on each neighbouring epithelial cell. This matters at junctions where three or more cells meet. For a node \(n_1\) and a candidate face of a neighbouring epithelial cell, the candidate partners are the three nodes of the face; a node is excluded if

  • the normal of \(n_1\) does not point against the normal of the face;

  • its normal does not point against the normal of \(n_1\) (\(\hat{\mathbf n}_1 \cdot \hat{\mathbf n}_2 > 0\));

  • its mean curvature exceeds \(H_{\max}\) of its cell type;

  • \(n_1\) is already coupled to a closer node of that cell, or the candidate is already coupled to a closer node of the cell of \(n_1\).

The closest remaining node within \(c_a\) becomes the partner of \(n_1\) for that neighbouring cell, in both directions. Each node keeps at most one partner per neighbouring cell. A node and all its partners are moved to their mean position, and the time integration moves the group as one node with averaged force, momentum and mass. As in model 1, uncoupled pairs only repel, with range \(\max(c_a, c_r)\), and \(\omega\) is not used.

Changed in version 2.1: The current partner of a node is now looked up by the local id of the neighbouring cell, the key under which it is stored. In 1.0 it was looked up by the global cell id, which differs from the local id once cells have divided, so the partner was whichever candidate a thread visited last rather than the nearest node (#113). The closest-partner rule is now applied under the locks of both nodes, both directions of a coupling are stored together, and repulsion forces on shared nodes use an atomic add. These fixes change results compared with 1.0.

Changed in version 2.0: For a node that projects onto the interior of a triangle, 1.0 measured the distance to a wrong point (a + ab*v + a + ac*w, one vertex a too many), so the cutoff tests missed contacts in face interiors. The distance is now measured to the closest point. This affects all three models.

Mean curvature at the nodes#

Models 1 and 2 need a normal and a mean curvature at every node. After the internal forces of a cell are computed, each node \(i\) receives the cotangent-Laplacian mean curvature

(2)#\[ H_i = \frac{1}{4 A_i} \left\lVert \sum_{j} (\cot\alpha_{ij} + \cot\beta_{ij}) (\mathbf r_j - \mathbf r_i) \right\rVert , \]

where the sum runs over the neighbours \(j\) of \(i\), \(\alpha_{ij}\) and \(\beta_{ij}\) are the angles opposite the edge \(ij\), and \(A_i\) is an area of about one third of the faces around \(i\). \(H_i\) is an unsigned value in 1/m. The node normal is the area-weighted mean of the face normals; where \(H_i\) exceeds the curvature of a sphere of the cell’s volume, \(1/R\) with \(R = (3V/4\pi)^{1/3}\), the direction of the mean-curvature vector is used instead. The values are those of the previous iteration when contacts are detected.

\(H_{\max}\) lets strongly curved parts of the surface, such as the rim of a contact, detach: no node with \(H_i > H_{\max}\) is coupled, and such a node is not repelled either when it is the touching node (see Model 1).

Contact detection algorithms#

The search for candidate node-face pairs (the broad phase) is chosen in the parameter file with the optional tag contact_detection_algorithm. It is used only by contact model 2; models 0 and 1 always use the uniform grid.

Value

Method

uspg (default)

Uniform grid. Model 2 bins the face boxes in a compact grid whose voxel edge is the mean box size, at most \(3\,l_{\min} + 2c\), and splits the nodes of very large cells into several work units. Models 0 and 1 use the USPG with voxel edge \(3\,l_{\min} + 2c\).

sweep_and_prune

Sweep-and-Prune over the bounding boxes of the cells; the faces of overlapping cells are then filtered by their own boxes.

adaptive

The uniform grid below 500 cells, Sweep-and-Prune from 500 cells on.

The contact rules are the same with all three; the choice is about run time (Parallel performance). If the bounding box of the tissue is no longer finite, or the grid would need more voxels than it can index, the run stops with an error instead of hanging (Safeguards).

Practical guidance#

  • Repulsion has a maximum stress. The repulsive stress grows as \(\xi d\) only up to the range of the law, so the largest repulsive stress is about \(\xi\,c\). With the Table 2 values (\(\xi = 10^{9}\) N/m³, \(c = 2\times10^{-7}\) m) this is 200 Pa. Where cells press on each other with a larger pressure (see Cell mechanics), repulsion cannot hold them apart and they can pass through each other.

  • No restoring force beyond the cutoff. A node is repelled by a face only while its distance \(d\) to the closest point of that face is below the range (\(c_r\) in model 0, \(\max(c_a, c_r)\) in models 1 and 2). A node pushed deeper into a neighbour, farther than the range from every face of that neighbour, receives no contact force at all, so two cells that have passed into each other stay superposed (cell superposition). In the November 2026 benchmark, the core of the lab’s growth benchmark (contact model 1, cutoff \(5\times10^{-7}\) m) came to hold superposed cells, two meshes in almost the same place, in every version tested, 1.0 included. In the 2.1.0 runs this began between 4,000 and 13,000 cells; above about 5,000 cells, treat the per-cell statistics of that tissue as valid only in its outer shell (Limits of the model).

  • Cells interpenetrate in long growth runs of the reconstructed paper-like configuration parameters/longrun_fast_growth.xml, with 2.1 and with 1.0. The CHANGELOG lists this as a known issue. In that file the contact cutoff (\(2\times10^{-7}\) m) is five times smaller than the minimum edge length (\(10^{-6}\) m), which may be one cause; the paper’s Table 2 uses \(2\times10^{-7}\) m for both, and the file is a reconstruction for 2.x, not the lab’s configuration (Tissue growth from a single cell). Check the overlap of cells in the output when the geometry of contacts matters.

  • Adhesion in models 1 and 2 exists only between epithelial cells. Contacts with lumen, ECM, nucleus and static cells are purely repulsive. \(\omega\) has an effect only with model 0.

  • Shipped values. Most epithelial face types use \(\omega = \xi = 10^{9}\) N/m³ on apical and lateral faces and \(\omega = 0\), \(\xi = 5\times10^{8}\) N/m³ on basal faces; ECM, lumen and nucleus faces use \(\xi\) between \(10^{9}\) and \(5\times10^{9}\) N/m³. Cutoffs are \(2\times10^{-7}\) to \(5\times10^{-7}\) m and \(H_{\max}\) is \(2.5\times10^{6}\) or \(5\times10^{6}\) 1/m for epithelial cells.

  • Thread safety. Models 0 and 1 add contact forces to the nodes of other cells with a plain, non-atomic add, inside a loop that runs in parallel over cells; two threads that add to the same node at once can lose one of the two forces, and no warning is printed (#120). These are the two models described in the paper. Run them with --threads=1 where exact forces matter. Model 2 uses an atomic add and is not affected. The size of the error has not been measured: the multi-threaded growth benchmark runs of November 2026 (contact model 1) repeated to within about 2%, which bounds the scatter it adds but not a bias (Reproducibility). Multi-threaded runs of any model are not bit-reproducible.

  • Stale links. When a node switches to a closer partner, its old partner keeps a link back to it until the couplings are rebuilt at the next iteration. This behaviour is present since 1.0.

  • The contact model is a compile-time choice; a parameter file written for one model gives a different simulation with another (Compile-time configuration).
